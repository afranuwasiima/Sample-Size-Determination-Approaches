01 — Why Sample Size Matters

Choosing the Right Formula Before You Choose a Number

What is sample size determination — in plain language?

When you run a survey or evaluation, you rarely have the resources to reach every single person in your target population. Instead, you select a sample — a smaller group that represents the whole. Sample size determination is the process of figuring out how many people you need to include so that what you learn from the sample can be trusted as a fair reflection of the entire population.

Think of it this way: if you want to know what percentage of households in a district use a mosquito net, you could visit all 50,000 households — or you could visit 400 carefully selected ones and be 95% confident your answer is within ±5% of the truth. The formulas in this guide tell you exactly how many "400" is for your specific situation.

But here’s the catch: the number changes depending on your study design. Are you estimating a single percentage, or comparing two groups? Is your outcome a yes/no question, or a measurement like weight? Are you selecting households directly from a register, or going village by village? Each scenario needs a different formula. Using the wrong one is like measuring distance with a kitchen scale — the tool doesn’t match the task.

From my published work: three examples of sample size in action

Approach 3 — Two-Arm RCT

In our RCT testing a mobile phone application for SRH services among university students in Uganda, we needed to power the study to detect a meaningful difference in SRH uptake between intervention and control arms. We used a two-arm power calculation with a specified minimum detectable difference, 80% power, and 95% confidence — exactly Approach 3 in this guide.

Nuwamanya, Nuwasiima et al. (2018) Reproductive Health, 15(1), 216. 32 citations

Approach 4 — Quasi-Experimental

For a quasi-experimental study of family planning benefits cards among urban youth in Kampala slums, we designed a stratified beneficiary sample across programme sites with known cluster sizes. The sampling frame came from a defined beneficiary register — making Approach 4 (beneficiary cluster enumeration) the correct choice over simple random sampling.

Nuwasiima et al. (2017) Reproductive Health, 14(1), 140. 12 citations

Approach 9 — Multi-Arm α Adjustment

In an immunisation demand generation experiment, we wanted to test 3 different interventions against our standard of care — and compare each arm against the others. With multiple pairwise comparisons, a standard α=0.05 would have inflated our false-positive rate well beyond 5%. We had to adjust alpha by the number of potential comparisons using Approach 9 (Šidák correction) before estimating sample size per arm.

Sample size is not a single number — it is the output of a set of design decisions. The most common mistake in health and development research is applying the Cochran formula to every study regardless of design, then defending an arbitrary number as “statistically sound.” The formula you choose must match how you will collect data, what you are trying to measure, and what comparisons you intend to make.

This guide covers nine scientifically distinct approaches. Each is appropriate for a different study type. The decision tree below helps you select the right one.

P
Prevalence / Proportion

The estimated value of your key indicator at baseline. Using an empirical, district-specific value (rather than the conservative 50%) reduces sample size by up to 25% with full scientific justification.

e / d
Margin of Error

How precise does your estimate need to be? At ±5% your n doubles vs ±7%. In resource-constrained humanitarian settings, ±7–8% is widely accepted.

ρ
Intracluster Correlation

When households are sampled in clusters (villages, blocks), observations within the same cluster are more similar to each other. ρ=0.05 is the Uganda Demographic and Health Survey (UDHS) average across 48 surveys. Consult your country’s Demographic and Health Survey (DHS) or national household survey for local ICC values.

DEFF
Design Effect

The inflation factor applied to your base sample size when using cluster sampling. DEFF = 1 + ρ(m–1). At ρ=0.05 and m=10, DEFF=1.45 — you need 45% more households than simple random sampling.

1–β
Statistical Power

Only relevant for comparative studies (impact evaluations). Power = probability of detecting a true effect. 80% is the minimum standard. Not applicable to descriptive baselines.

NR
Non-Response Buffer

A practical inflation to account for households that refuse, are absent, or are ineligible. 5% is standard for community surveys with good access. Inflate to 10–15% in hard-to-reach settings.

FPC
Finite Population Correction

When sampling >5% of a known population N, the standard formula overestimates variance. FPC = √((N−n)/(N−1)). Apply nadj = n×N / (n+N−1). Always use for beneficiary populations under ~10,000.

Numeric / Continuous Outcome Parameters

μ
Population Mean

For continuous outcomes (MUAC, hemoglobin, test scores, income). The target value you want to estimate or compare. Sample size depends on variability (σ), not proportions.

σ
Standard Deviation

The spread of your outcome in the population. From prior studies, pilot data, or literature. Larger σ = more variability = larger sample needed. The single most critical numeric parameter.

δ
Detectable Difference

Minimum meaningful difference between group means (e.g. 0.5 kg weight gain, 5 score points). Expressed in the outcome's own units. Smaller δ = much larger sample required.

e
Absolute Precision (Means)

For one-sample estimation: how close do you need your estimated mean to the true mean? e.g. ±0.5 kg, ±2 mmol/L, ±5 score points. Analogous to margin of error for proportions.

Proportion vs. Numeric outcomes: The Cochran formula assumes a binary (yes/no) outcome. If your primary indicator is continuous — MUAC in mm, hemoglobin in g/dL, knowledge score out of 100 — use Approaches 6–8 instead. Applying a proportion formula to a continuous outcome is a methodological error that will be flagged at peer review or IRB.
// Decision rule: which approach to use?

// ── PROPORTION / BINARY OUTCOMES ──────────────────────
IF descriptive baseline OR prevalence survey → Approach 1 (Cochran)
IF cluster-based field enumeration → Approach 2 (Cluster-Adjusted)
IF comparing treatment vs. control groups → Approach 3 (Two-Arm)
IF surveying program beneficiaries by strata → Approach 4 (Beneficiary)
IF surveying general population by strata → Approach 5 (General Population)

// ── NUMERIC / CONTINUOUS OUTCOMES ─────────────────────
IF estimating a mean (weight, score, distance, cost) → Approach 6 (One-Sample Mean)
IF comparing means between two groups → Approach 7 (Two-Sample Means)
IF cluster sampling with a continuous outcome → Approach 8 (Cluster Mean)

// ── FINITE POPULATION CORRECTION ─────────────────────
IF n/N > 0.05 → n_adj = n × N / (n + N − 1)
// Equivalent to: n_adj = n / (1 + (n−1)/N)

// ── ADJUSTMENTS ──────────────────────────────────────
IF sampling >5% of a known population N → Apply Finite Population Correction (Approaches 1, 6)
IF 3+ treatment arms with multiple comparisons → Approach 9 (Multi-Arm + α adjustment)
IF multiple primary outcomes → Calculate n for each, use the largest
IF subgroup analysis planned → Multiply n by number of subgroups requiring precision
Preferred Parameters for This Organisation

Margin of error: ±5% (e = 0.05)  ·  Confidence level: 95% (Z = 1.96)  ·  Statistical power: 80% (1–β = 0.80, for comparative designs)  ·  Non-response buffer: 5% (standard) to 10–15% (hard-to-reach)

All calculators below default to these values. Deviations must be justified in your methods section.

Why Plot Scenarios?

Sample size determination is rarely a single calculation — it is a negotiation between scientific rigour and operational feasibility. Running multiple scenarios helps you find the point where statistical validity meets budget, logistics, and timeline constraints.

Scientific Rigour

Tighter precision (±5%) and higher power (90%) produce more defensible estimates. But they require larger samples — sometimes 2–3× the "good enough" scenario. When publishing in peer-reviewed journals or submitting to an IRB, the higher-rigour scenario is what reviewers expect.

Operational Feasibility

Budget caps, enumerator availability, geographic access, and survey duration often set an upper bound on sample size. A ±7–8% scenario may be the largest you can field. Plotting both scenarios side by side lets you present the trade-off transparently to funders and programme teams.

Each calculator below includes a scenario comparison or sensitivity table. Use them to present 2–3 options in your protocol: a preferred (high-rigour) scenario, a feasible (budget-fit) alternative, and the minimum acceptable threshold. Document the rationale for your final choice in the methods section.

Approach 01

Simple Prevalence (Cochran Formula)

USE WHEN → Descriptive baseline · Single-arm prevalence survey · Direct household selection from a list

Cross-sectional surveys estimating the prevalence of a single indicator

The Cochran (1977) formula estimates the minimum sample needed to estimate a proportion P within a specified margin of error, at a given confidence level. It assumes simple random sampling — no clustering, no stratification beyond what is explicitly modeled. This is the appropriate formula when you have access to a household register (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR), Office of the Prime Minister (OPM), Uganda Bureau of Statistics (UBOS)) and can select households directly without village-level clustering.

n = Z² × P(1–P) / e²
// Z = Z-score for confidence level (1.96 for 95% CI)
// P = estimated prevalence of key indicator
// e = desired margin of error (precision)

n_final = CEILING( n / (1 – NR), 1 )
// NR = non-response rate (e.g. 0.05 for 5%)
⚠ Using P=0.50 is the most conservative assumption and maximises sample size. If you have a district-specific or national estimate for your key indicator, use it — this reduces n by up to 25% with full scientific justification. Always cite your source.
e.g. 0.742 for ANC4 coverage 74.2% (2024 AHSPR, Adjumani). At ±5% with P=0.742: n≈296.
±5% preferred. 0.05 = ±5%, 0.07 = ±7%, 0.10 = ±10%
0.05 = 5% buffer. Use 0.10–0.15 for hard-to-reach settings.
Enter known population size. Leave 0 to skip FPC. FPC is applied when n/N > 5%.

Approach 02

Cluster-Adjusted Sample Size

USE WHEN → Household selection via villages/blocks · Refugee settlement enumeration · No complete household list available

Single-arm descriptive survey using cluster (two-stage) sampling

When households cannot be selected directly — common in refugee settlements where UNHCR/OPM registers are organized by block or village — a two-stage cluster approach is used: first select clusters (villages, enumeration areas), then select households within each cluster. This introduces intracluster correlation (households in the same village are more similar), which inflates the effective sample size via the Design Effect (DEFF). Ignoring DEFF in a clustered design produces artificially narrow confidence intervals and underpowered estimates.

DEFF = 1 + ρ(m – 1)
// ρ = intracluster correlation coefficient (ICC)
// m = cluster size (households per cluster)

n_cluster = [Z² × P(1–P) / e²] × DEFF
n_final = CEILING( n_cluster / (1 – NR), 1 )
C = CEILING( n_final / m, 1 )
// C = number of clusters needed
UDHS Uganda average: 0.04–0.07 across 48 surveys. Use 0.05 as default.
Households per cluster. 5–15 is operationally standard.

Approach 03

Two-Arm Impact Evaluation

USE WHEN → RCT or quasi-experimental design · Treatment vs. control comparison · Pre-post with comparator group

Comparative study requiring statistical power to detect a minimum detectable difference

Impact evaluations compare an intervention group to a control group, requiring power to detect a true effect if one exists. This requires specifying both a confidence level (α) and statistical power (1–β). The minimum detectable difference (MDD) is the smallest effect you want to reliably detect. A pre-specified design effect accounts for clustering at the district or village level. This approach produces significantly larger samples than single-arm designs and is not appropriate for descriptive baselines.

n_per_arm = DEFF × [Z_α/2 + Z_β]² × [P₁(1–P₁) + P₂(1–P₂)] / (P₁ – P₂)²
// P₁ = baseline prevalence, P₂ = P₁ + MDD (expected endline)
// Z_α/2 = 1.96 (95% CI), Z_β = 0.84 (80% power)
// DEFF applied per arm (e.g. 1.22 from DHS immunization indicator)
n_total = 2 × n_per_arm (one per arm)
n_final = CEILING( n_total / (1 – NR), 1 )
⚠ Power (1–β) is only relevant here. Do not include power in a descriptive baseline formula — it is not applicable and will mislead reviewers.
e.g. 0.569 for FIC baseline, 0.20 for dropout rate
Expected change from baseline to endline. e.g. 0.10 = 10 percentage points
1.22 = DHS estimate for immunization coverage indicator

Approach 04

Beneficiary Survey (Cluster Enumeration Area)

USE WHEN → Surveying program beneficiaries · National statistical body enumeration areas as clusters (e.g. UBOS in Uganda) · Known strata (e.g. program offices)

Stratified cluster sample for beneficiary population using standard error parameterization

This approach uses the number of clusters (enumeration areas) as the primary output, parameterized by standard error rather than margin of error. It is appropriate when sampling from a defined beneficiary population organized in known spatial units (national statistical body enumeration areas, program zones), and when the primary interest is ensuring adequate representation across strata rather than achieving a specific prevalence precision.

C = P(1–P) × DEFF / (SE² × b)
// C = number of clusters (enumeration areas)
// b = cluster size (persons per EA)
// SE = standard error (not margin of error)
// DEFF = design effect (typically 2 for beneficiary surveys)
n = C × b (total sample)
n_strata = n × strata (total across all program offices/zones)
Use 0.5 for maximum sample size when multiple indicators tracked.
Not margin of error. SE = e/Z (e.g. SE≈0.025 corresponds to ±5% at 95% CI)
Households or persons per enumeration area
DEFF=2 is standard for beneficiary surveys without prior ICC data
e.g. 10 program offices + 1 sub-region = 11. Adapt strata to your country context.

Approach 05

General Population Survey

USE WHEN → Surveying non-beneficiary or general population · Known population-level prevalence · Stratified by region or program zone

Stratified cluster sample for general population using known prevalence and standard error

Identical in structure to Approach 4 but uses a population-level prevalence estimate for the key outcome, rather than defaulting to P=0.50. This is appropriate when surveying the general population (not just program beneficiaries) where prior data exists on outcome prevalence. The lower P reduces required sample size, but the DEFF and stratification structure remain the same.

C = P(1–P) × DEFF / (SE² × b)
n = C × b × strata

Approach 06 — Numeric Outcome

One-Sample Mean Estimation

USE WHEN → Estimating a population mean · Single continuous indicator · No comparison group · e.g. mean MUAC, mean hemoglobin, mean knowledge score

Cross-sectional survey to estimate a single population mean within a specified absolute precision

When your primary outcome is a continuous (numeric) variable — weight, height, score, income, distance — you cannot use the Cochran proportion formula. Instead, sample size is driven by the population standard deviation (σ) and the absolute precision (e) you need around your estimated mean. The key challenge is obtaining a reliable estimate of σ from prior studies, pilot data, or published literature for a similar population.

n = (Z × σ / e)²
// Z = Z-score for confidence level (1.96 for 95% CI)
// σ = population standard deviation of the outcome
// e = desired absolute precision (in same units as outcome)
// e.g. if MUAC in mm, e=5 means estimate within ±5mm of true mean

n_final = CEILING( n / (1 – NR), 1 )
⚠ σ is the hardest parameter to estimate for numeric outcomes. Use: (1) a prior study in the same population, (2) a small pilot survey (n=20–30), or (3) the empirical rule: σ ≈ Range/4 if you know the plausible min/max of the outcome. Always document your σ source — reviewers will ask.
In same units as outcome. e.g. σ=1.5 for hemoglobin in g/dL; σ=12 for MUAC in mm; σ=15 for score out of 100.
How close to the true mean? Same units as σ. e.g. ±0.5 g/dL hemoglobin; ±5mm MUAC; ±5 score points.

Approach 07 — Numeric Outcome

Two-Sample Means Comparison

USE WHEN → Comparing mean outcomes between intervention vs. control · Pre-post with comparator group · Continuous primary endpoint in an impact evaluation

Powered comparison of means between two independent groups with a minimum detectable difference

When your impact evaluation tests whether an intervention changes a continuous outcome (e.g. mean hemoglobin increases, mean MUAC improves, mean knowledge score rises), you need statistical power to detect a specified minimum difference (δ) between group means. This formula requires both a confidence level (α) and power (1–β), and produces one sample size per arm. The allocation ratio (k) allows unequal group sizes — common when control communities are fewer or more expensive to access.

n₁ = (Z_α + Z_β)² × σ² × (1 + 1/k) / δ²
// n₁ = sample size for intervention arm
// σ = pooled standard deviation (assume equal across arms)
// δ = minimum detectable difference between means
// k = allocation ratio (n₁/n₂); k=1 for equal groups
// Z_α = 1.96 (95% CI), Z_β = 0.842 (80% power)

n₂ = n₁ / k
n_total = CEILING( (n₁ + n₂) / (1 – NR), 1 )
Effect size conventions (Cohen's d = δ/σ): Small = 0.2, Medium = 0.5, Large = 0.8. In development and education research, a detectable effect of d=0.3–0.5 is typical. For nutrition interventions in East Africa, δ=0.5 g/dL hemoglobin with σ=1.5 gives d=0.33 (small-medium). Always justify δ from programmatic significance, not statistical convention alone.
⚠ δ must be the minimum clinically/programmatically meaningful difference — not just any detectable difference. Too-small δ inflates sample size massively. Consult subject-matter experts: a 0.5 g/dL hemoglobin difference may be statistically detectable but clinically irrelevant. A 1.0 g/dL difference is the standard threshold in many nutrition studies.
Assumed equal across arms. From prior data or pilot.
Smallest meaningful difference between group means. Same units as σ.
1 = equal groups. 2 = twice as many in intervention arm.

Approach 08 — Numeric Outcome

Cluster-Adjusted Mean Estimation

USE WHEN → Continuous outcome + cluster-based field sampling · Villages/blocks as primary sampling unit · e.g. mean MUAC survey by cluster, mean score across schools

One-sample mean estimation with design effect adjustment for cluster sampling

Combines Approach 6 (mean estimation) with Approach 2 (cluster adjustment). When households or individuals are sampled by cluster (village, school, block) rather than directly, the design effect inflates the simple random sample size required. The ICC (ρ) and cluster size (m) together determine the DEFF. This is the correct approach for nutrition surveys in refugee settlements where individuals are grouped in household blocks, or for school-based assessments where classrooms are the sampling unit.

DEFF = 1 + ρ(m – 1)
n_base = (Z × σ / e)² // same as Approach 6
n_cluster = n_base × DEFF
n_final = CEILING( n_cluster / (1 – NR), 1 )
C = CEILING( n_final / m, 1 ) // number of clusters
For continuous outcomes, ρ is often lower than for binary outcomes. 0.02–0.05 is typical for anthropometric measures.

Approach 09 — Multi-Arm Correction

Multi-Arm Trial with Alpha Adjustment

USE WHEN → 3+ treatment arms compared to control · Multiple pairwise comparisons · Factorial designs · Dose-finding studies · Multi-site evaluations with site-level comparisons

Sample size per arm with Bonferroni or Šidák correction for familywise Type I error control

When an impact evaluation compares more than two groups (e.g. intervention A vs. control, intervention B vs. control, intervention A vs. B), each pairwise comparison inflates the probability of a false positive. If you conduct k comparisons at α=0.05 each, the familywise error rate rises to approximately 1–(1–α)k. For k=3 comparisons, this is 14.3% — nearly three times the intended 5%. Alpha adjustment corrects each individual test to maintain the overall familywise α at the desired level.

// BONFERRONI CORRECTION
α_adj = α / k
// α = desired familywise error rate (e.g. 0.05)
// k = number of pairwise comparisons
// For 3 arms vs. control: k = 3 comparisons
// For 4 arms total (all pairwise): k = C(4,2) = 6

// ŠIDÁK CORRECTION (less conservative)
α_adj = 1 – (1 – α)^(1/k)
// Exact correction; less conservative than Bonferroni

// SAMPLE SIZE PER ARM (proportion outcome)
Z_adj = Z-score at α_adj/2 (two-sided)
n_per_arm = DEFF × [Z_adj + Z_β]² × [P₁(1–P₁) + P₂(1–P₂)] / (P₁–P₂)²
n_total = n_per_arm × (arms + 1) // +1 for shared control
n_final = CEILING( n_total / (1 – NR), 1 )
⚠ When to adjust: Alpha adjustment is required whenever you plan multiple primary comparisons from the same study. It is not required for (a) a single pre-specified primary comparison with secondary/exploratory analyses, (b) subgroup analyses declared as exploratory, or (c) a single comparison across multiple outcomes if a primary outcome is pre-specified.
Choosing a correction: Bonferroni divides α equally among k comparisons — always valid but conservative. Šidák is slightly less conservative and exact for independent tests. Holm-Bonferroni (sequential) offers better power but applies at the analysis stage only. For sample size calculation, use Bonferroni (conservative) or Šidák (recommended).

Methodological Rigour

Common Mistakes in Sample Size Determination

These errors recur in grant proposals, IRB submissions, and published studies. Each one undermines statistical validity and can invalidate findings at peer review.

Mistake 1
Using Cochran's formula for everything

The Cochran formula (Approach 1) assumes simple random sampling and a binary outcome. Applying it to a clustered design produces artificially narrow confidence intervals. Applying it to a continuous outcome (e.g. MUAC, hemoglobin) is a methodological error — use Approaches 6–8 instead. Applying it to a two-arm comparison without power analysis makes the study underpowered.

Mistake 2
Ignoring the design effect in clustered sampling

If you select households via villages, schools, or enumeration areas, your effective sample size is smaller than the raw count. Ignoring DEFF means your confidence intervals are too narrow and your p-values are too small. This is the single most common statistical error in health surveys in low-resource settings.

Mistake 3
Defaulting to P = 0.50 without justification

P = 0.50 maximises sample size and is conservative, but it is not always necessary or appropriate. If you have a prior estimate (from DHIS2, DHS, UBOS, or a pilot), use it. Cite the source. Reviewers flag "P = 0.50 for maximum sample" as lazy when prior data exists. The reduction can be 20–25% with full scientific justification.

Mistake 4
Confusing margin of error with standard error

Margin of error (e) = Z × SE. Standard error (SE) = e / Z. At 95% CI: SE = e / 1.96. Approach 4 (Beneficiary) uses SE directly; Approaches 1–3 use e. Mixing them up inflates or deflates sample size by a factor of ~4. Always check which parameterization your formula expects.

Mistake 5
Including power in a descriptive baseline

Statistical power (1−β) is only relevant for hypothesis-testing designs: RCTs, quasi-experiments, pre-post with comparator. A descriptive prevalence survey has no hypothesis to test and no effect to detect. Adding power to a baseline formula inflates sample size unnecessarily and signals methodological confusion to reviewers.

Mistake 6
Omitting finite population correction for small populations

When sampling more than 5% of a known population (e.g. 200 out of 2,000 beneficiaries), the FPC reduces the required sample size. nadj = n × N / (n + N − 1). Omitting FPC means over-sampling and wasting resources. Conversely, not using it when n/N > 5% produces overly conservative estimates.

Mistake 7
Using an undocumented σ for continuous outcomes

For mean-based sample sizes (Approaches 6–8), σ is the most critical parameter. Reviewers will ask: "Where does your σ come from?" Acceptable sources: (1) a prior study in the same population, (2) a pilot survey (n ≥ 20), (3) the empirical rule σ ≈ Range/4, (4) published literature for a similar population. Guessing σ is unacceptable.

Mistake 8
Not adjusting for multiple primary outcomes

If your study has multiple primary indicators, calculate sample size for each and use the largest. If you plan multiple hypothesis tests, consider Bonferroni or other multiplicity corrections — otherwise, Type I error accumulates. State your primary outcome clearly; secondary outcomes should not drive sample size.

Sample Size Reporting Checklist

Every methods section should document how the sample size was determined. Use this checklist to ensure your report meets IRB, journal, and funder requirements. Based on CONSORT, STROBE, and WHO reporting guidelines.

// ── MINIMUM REPORTING REQUIREMENTS ─────────────────────
☐ Study design type (cross-sectional, RCT, quasi-experimental, cohort)
☐ Primary outcome variable and whether it is binary or continuous
☐ Formula used, with full citation (Cochran, Fleiss, WHO, etc.)
☐ All parameter values used: P or σ, e or δ, Z, power (if applicable)
☐ Source of each parameter (cite DHS, DHIS2, pilot, published literature)
☐ Non-response rate assumption and justification

// ── IF CLUSTER SAMPLING ────────────────────────────────
☐ ICC (ρ) value and source
☐ Cluster size (m) and how it was determined
☐ Design effect (DEFF) calculation shown
☐ Number of clusters required
☐ Sampling frame description (national statistical body EAs (e.g. UBOS in Uganda, KNBS in Kenya), UNHCR blocks, school register)

// ── IF COMPARATIVE / IMPACT DESIGN ─────────────────────
☐ Statistical power level (80%, 85%, 90%) and justification
☐ Minimum detectable difference/effect (MDD or δ) and justification
☐ Allocation ratio (if unequal arms)
☐ Whether the test is one-sided or two-sided

// ── IF FINITE POPULATION ───────────────────────────────
☐ Population size (N) and source
☐ Whether FPC was applied and the adjusted sample size
☐ Sampling fraction (n/N) reported

// ── FINAL CHECKS ──────────────────────────────────────
☐ Final sample size clearly stated (total and per arm/stratum)
☐ Sensitivity analysis shown (at least one alternate scenario)
☐ Budget and feasibility constraints acknowledged
☐ Software or tool used for calculation identified
⚠ CONSORT (2010) requires RCTs to report: "How sample size was determined. When applicable, explanation of any interim analyses and stopping guidelines."
